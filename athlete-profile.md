## Coaching philosophy

Built 2026-07-23 from: (1) real research into 3 coaches/schools the athlete resonates with, (2) a direct interview, (3) intervals.icu training data (Strava blocked by API subscription wall). This is a synthesis, not a copy of any single influence.

### The three influences researched

**Olav Aleksander Bu** (coach of Kristian Blummenfelt & Gustav Iden — Norwegian triathlon method). Pyramidal volume structure where intensity drops as volume rises; double-threshold days split across the day (e.g. bike threshold AM, run threshold PM) with recovery meals between; every session's intensity is set and adjusted from actual lactate/data, not feel; carbohydrate intake pushed well above old assumed ceilings to support the volume. [Triathlete — Norwegian Method](https://www.triathlete.com/ironman-world-championship-2024-kona/the-norwegian-method-for-trash-talking-according-to-kristian-blummenfelt-and-gustav-iden/), [TrainingPeaks — Norwegian Training Method](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/norwegian-training-method-world-champion-triathletes/), [Triathlete — Bu on fueling](https://www.triathlete.com/culture/news/why-kristian-blummenfelt-counts-calories/), [TriWorldHub — Norwegian Method Periodization](https://triworldhub.com/norwegian-method-periodization/).

**Marius Bakken** (originator of double-threshold training for runners, Norwegian 5000m record holder). Threshold work is "broken" into intervals with short rest instead of one continuous tempo, to bank more time at a fast-but-controlled effort; the ceiling is deliberately kept just *under* threshold (historically lactate 2.3-3.5 mmol/L) — go too hard and the adaptation drops off; easy days are kept genuinely easy, no "stuff" added to them; the core belief is "you don't improve by training as hard as possible, you improve by training hard enough, often enough, for long enough." [Running Writings — Bakken in his own words](https://runningwritings.com/2024/09/marius-bakken-double-threshold.html), [mariusbakken.com — The Norwegian Model](https://www.mariusbakken.com/the-norwegian-model.html), [Coach Saltmarsh — Bakken masterclass](https://coachsaltmarsh.com/articles/marius-bakken-double-threshold/).

**Arthur Lydiard** (father of modern periodization). Everything is planned backwards from race day: a long aerobic base phase first (his line: "you can't sharpen a knife that hasn't been forged"), then hills for strength, then track/sharpening work, then taper. The base phase itself isn't slow jogging — much of it runs close to aerobic threshold. [Science of Running — Lydiard](https://www.scienceofrunning.com/2016/11/arthur-lydiard-the-father-of-modern-training.html), [Athletics Illustrated — 5 Lydiard principles](https://athleticsillustrated.com/the-five-lydiard-principles-explained/), [The Endurance Collective — cascade of aerobic efforts](https://www.theendurancecollective.com/the-endurance-collected/2022/10/11/the-key-to-a-strong-base-lydiards-cascade-of-aerobic-efforts).

**Where they agree:** all three build a big aerobic foundation before adding sharp work, control intensity precisely rather than "racing" training sessions, and treat easy days as genuinely easy so hard days can be genuinely hard.

**Where they genuinely disagree:** Bu and Bakken build *speed* through controlled sub-threshold volume done often (multiple threshold touches per week, sometimes per day); Lydiard builds speed only after months of pure aerobic base, then adds hills and track work in a separate, later phase. Bakken is almost dogmatic about staying *under* threshold; Bu's triathlon volumes push total daily load higher than either running-only model would consider sane, because the load is spread across 3 disciplines.

### The synthesis — this athlete's approach
1. **Lydiard base, kept.** The big low-intensity aerobic foundation stays the backbone, especially outside dedicated prep blocks — it's both scientifically sound and the thing that's already working.
2. **One weekly threshold touch, Bakken-style, not full Bu-style double-threshold.** True double-threshold (2x/day) is built for pros training 20+ hours a week; at this athlete's real weekly budget, the adaptation is a single broken-threshold session (e.g. intervals held just under LTHR, controlled by pace/HR since there's no lactate meter) — enough to close the "no fractionné in 2 years" gap the athlete flagged and the data confirmed, without adding a second daily session that logistics can't support.
3. **Hills/VMA: light touch, front-loaded, not a staple.** The athlete dislikes them and finds threshold/tempo work more enjoyable and sustainable — clarified 2026-07-23: a bit of VMA/hills early in a block (base phase) is fine if useful, he'll do it, just don't make it a recurring weekly fixture through the whole prep. This also lines up with Lydiard, who puts hill work in its own early phase rather than carrying it all the way to race day. Threshold work stays the default format (broken-tempo, flat/rolling terrain) once the block gets marathon-specific.
4. **Sunday long tempo stays sacred.** It's the favorite session, it's a genuine strength in the data, and it lines up with both Lydiard's long-run tradition and Bakken/Bu's belief in controlled sub-threshold volume as the main speed-builder.
5. **Data-driven, not feel-driven, pacing.** HR zones and pace zones (see Zones/paces above) set the ceiling for every session, echoing the lactate-controlled precision Bu and Bakken both rely on — adapted to this athlete's tools (HR/pace, not lactate strips).
6. **Volume is the one thing to guard like a hawk.** Given the athlete's own confirmed injury trigger, every plan holds week-over-week running volume near the ~10% guardrail (with its real limits — Nielsen et al. 2014 found no rule validated 10% specifically, and that single-session jumps, especially the long run, matter more than smooth weekly totals) and never trades that discipline away for a faster path to fitness.
7. **Flexible frame, not a fixed script.** Day-to-day sequencing adapts to the athlete's real week (per the plan-my-week skill); the philosophy sets the non-negotiables (protected Sunday long run, one threshold touch, volume discipline) and leaves the rest to be arranged around real life.
8. **Tone: direct on facts, kind in delivery.** Flag what the data actually shows — good or bad — without softening it, but never harshly.
